## 3. That multi_logloss = 2.64914 is a five-alarm fire

From training:

```text
Training until validation scores don't improve for 75 rounds
Did not meet early stopping. Best iteration is:
[900]   valid_0's multi_logloss: 2.64914
```

Let’s unpack that:

### 3.1. How bad is 2.65, in context?

For a 3-class problem, if you just always predict the class frequencies:

* p(0) ≈ 0.785
* p(+1) ≈ 0.120
* p(−1) ≈ 0.095

The expected multi-logloss (natural log) is just the entropy of that distribution. That works out to roughly:

> **Baseline “do nothing clever” logloss ≈ 0.67**

Your model at 2.65 is:

* Worse than a dumb frequency baseline by about **+1.98** logloss.
* That implies the model’s average probability on the *true* class is around `exp(-2.65) ≈ 7%`.

So the model is not “weakly useful.” It’s **actively bad** *or* the metric is being computed on the wrong labels / wrong mapping / wrong samples.

Random uniform probabilities across 3 classes give logloss ≈ 1.10.
You’re at 2.65. So the model is doing worse than random guessing, by a factor of several in cross-entropy space.

That’s why this is a structural red flag, not a “meh, could be better” situation.

### 3.2. What could cause such catastrophic logloss?

A few usual suspects:

1. **Label encoding mismatch with LightGBM**

   * Multi-class LightGBM expects labels in `{0, 1, 2}` when `num_class = 3`.
   * If you pass labels `{ -1, 0, 1 }` or remapped values but then interpret them as `{0, 1, 2}` inconsistently between training and evaluation, you can easily destroy logloss.
   * Example failure pattern:

     * Data stores labels as `{-1, 0, 1}`.
     * Some wrapper silently remaps them, but your evaluation code assumes a different order → probabilities are matched to the wrong class index.

2. **Massively skewed sample weights / custom objective**

   * If `targets_bigmove.yaml` or model config applies huge weights to certain examples (e.g. big losses) and the model can’t fit them, the **weighted** logloss can balloon.
   * In that case, unweighted logloss might be sane, but LightGBM reports the weighted version.

3. **Features / labels misaligned in time**

   * If your “aligned training data” is actually misaligned such that features at time t are paired with labels that belong to some other bar (e.g. wrong symbol/time shift), the model sees garbage:

     * It will not find stable relationships.
     * Probabilities will be close to nonsense and logloss becomes huge.
   * The fact that it happily runs to 900 iterations without early stopping just means:

     * It’s making *tiny* improvements on a fundamentally broken mapping; still ends up terrible.

4. **Using the wrong dataset for validation**

   * If `valid_0` is evaluating on some bizarre subset (e.g. extreme edge cases, or mis-filtered data) rather than a normal validation split, your reported logloss may not reflect the actual train/val behaviour.

5. **Bug in how predictions vs labels are aligned for metric calculation**

   * Even if labels and features are aligned, if you reorder rows or filter them between prediction and loss computation (without applying the same transformation to both), logloss is garbage.

Given your earlier run got **0.26** logloss on a smaller 3-class problem, jumping to **2.65** with a more sensible label distribution is not “oh, bigmove is harder.” It screams “wiring issue.”

## 4. How this lines up with your trading goal (3–5 sniper trades/day)

From a *pure PnL* perspective, you don’t care about logloss per se. But:

* When logloss is **this** bad, it usually means either:

  * The model is *not* learning any coherent mapping from features to labels.
  * Or you’re measuring the wrong thing.

Either way, the probabilities coming out of this model are very likely:

* Miscalibrated to the point of uselessness.
* Potentially inverted or systematically wrong.

If you then try to build a “3–5 trades/day” policy on top of those probabilities (top-K by `p_up` / `trade_score`), you risk:

* Trading the **worst** bars, not the best.
* Or at minimum, trading something close to random noise.

So before you even talk Sharpe / Sortino, you need to:

* Confirm that the model’s outputs are meaningfully related to realized returns at all.
* Fix the target/label/metric plumbing if they aren’t.

## 5. Concrete next steps (what I’d do next if I were forced to touch this codebase)

No questions, just a hit list:

1. **Inspect the label column in `training_data.parquet`**

   * Print:

     * Unique values.
     * Value counts.
   * Confirm labels are exactly `{0,1,2}` or `{−1,0,1}` and how the training script maps them for LightGBM.

2. **Check the LightGBM training call**

   * Confirm:

     * `objective='multiclass'` or `multiclassova` with `num_class=3`.
     * That the labels passed to LightGBM match what it expects.
   * If labels are `{-1,0,1}`, explicitly remap them to `{0,1,2}` and keep that mapping consistent in evaluation.

3. **Recompute a naive baseline logloss in code**

   * On the exact validation set used for `valid_0`, compute:

     * Baseline prediction = class frequencies.
     * Baseline logloss.
   * Compare: if baseline ≈ 0.7 and model at 2.65, you know it’s not just “hard data,” it’s broken logic.

4. **Verify time & symbol alignment**

   * Pick a random sample from the validation set:

     * Check that the label assigned to (symbol, timestamp) corresponds to the actual forward return based on the candles.
   * If you see systematic 1-bar shifts or mismatches, fix the alignment.

5. **Check `label_guard_report.json`**

   * See if it flags:

     * Extreme label skew by symbol/period.
     * NaNs, infinities, or anything suspicious.
   * Anything weird there is a clue.

6. **Only then** re-run training and look at:

   * New `valid_0 multi_logloss`.
   * More importantly: **tail PnL metrics** on validation/OOS (top-K trades/day).
